\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\input{figures/fig_teaser}

Humans have a remarkable ability to imagine what it would feel like to touch an object at a
particular location. Looking at a ceramic mug, we already know that its glazed side will feel
smooth and that the unglazed ring at its base will feel rough and gritty, long before our fingers
arrive there. This lets one plan a grasp or build a picture of the fine-grained surface detail of a
scene without physically touching every location. Importantly, the touch one imagines is not a
single snapshot in time: it is a mental simulation of how our non-rigid skin would progressively
deform during contact. Such imagination is possible because tactile information is highly
repetitive. Object surfaces are made of a small number of materials and recurring geometric motifs,
so a handful of touches already reveals most of what the rest of an object will feel like. What
remains is to work out which previously felt sensation applies at a new location, and how it should
be transformed to account for the change in surface geometry.

In this paper we aim to teach machines the same capacity. We introduce the task of finding
\emph{tactile analogies}: given a set of reference touches captured on an object, the goal is to
predict the tactile signal at new, untouched locations (Fig.~\ref{fig:teaser}). We represent a
touch as a \emph{tactile normal video}, a sequence of frames encoding the surface normals of the
visuo-tactile sensor's deformable gel over the duration of a press. Such videos are easy to obtain
in practice, as the mapping from the raw color measurements of a modern visuo-tactile sensor to gel
normals is by design nearly one-to-one, and manufacturers such as GelSight~\cite{gelsight} ship
color-to-normal conversion in their SDK. To predict gel normals at a new location, we render
surface normal maps at both the reference and query sensor poses and compare them to recover a
mapping that transfers reference tactile videos to the query location.

Our task therefore resonates with the analogy-based synthesis idea of Image
Analogies~\cite{imageanalogies}: observe a transformation on one pair of images, then predict how it
would unfold on a new input. Here the observed side is the normal map rendered at each sensor pose
and the target is a high-dimensional tactile video rather than a still image, so we ask for an
analogy between surface geometry and the temporal signal that arises through interaction with it.
This distinguishes our setting from prior example-based tactile synthesis, which predicts a single
tactile image at the moment of contact~\cite{tactiledreamfusion,tarf,objectfolder}. The task is
challenging on several counts: the gel deforms non-rigidly and its response depends on material
properties not observable from geometry alone, the search space is large as one must decide which
reference is relevant and how to map it onto the query pose, and there is no large-scale dataset of
paired touches on which a model could be trained end-to-end.

Our solution is therefore modular: retrieval and coarse prediction reduce the large search space,
and a learning-based refinement stage models gel dynamics. We first compare 2D foundation model
features~\cite{dinov3} between the reference normal renders and the query to obtain one promising
reference location. We then run pre-trained local feature matchers on the normal renderings, which
match accurately despite being out of distribution with respect to the multi-view RGB imagery they
were trained on. The matches give a homography that warps the reference video into an initial
transfer, which a refinement network edits to regress the ground-truth query video, learning the
gel deformation dynamics and correcting residual misalignment. Our experiments show that the
pipeline outperforms all tested baselines at a small runtime, and we outline potential applications
in 3D surface reconstruction and visuo-tactile sensor simulation.
