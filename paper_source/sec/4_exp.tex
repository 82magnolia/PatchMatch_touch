\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:exp}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

\paragraph{Implementation details.}
For retrieval we use a DINOv3 ViT-B/16 encoder~\cite{dinov3} on normal renderings at four times
the sensor footprint, resized to $224\times224$, and rank references by cosine similarity between
$\ell_2$-normalized class-token features. Coarse alignment uses SuperPoint
keypoints~\cite{superpoint} matched by SuperGlue~\cite{superglue} on the same $4\times$ normal
renderings, with a homography fitted by RANSAC~\cite{ransac} and a median translation offset
recovered at video resolution. The refinement network is the smallest ReBotNet
variant~\cite{rebotnet} (embedding widths $28/36/48/64$), taking a pair of $240\times320$ frames
plus the three concatenated query normal channels. We train for 20 epochs with AdamW, batch size
8, and learning rate $2\times10^{-4}$ with a cosine schedule. The best checkpoint
by validation PSNR occurs at epoch 18. Training runs on a single RTX 3090 GPU and takes
approximately 32 hours.

\paragraph{Benchmark.}
We create a benchmark for evaluating tactile analogies using the ObjectFolder
dataset~\cite{objectfolder}. For every object we generate a set of tactile normal videos recording
the gel normal changes over time, simulated with the Taxim tactile simulator~\cite{taxim} using a
scaled GelSight Mini calibration at $240\times320$ resolution. Each touch is a 50-frame video at
5\,fps following a press-in-then-withdraw depth schedule. Every touch is accompanied by
view-space renderings of the surface at the sensor pose, namely RGB colour, heightmap, surface
normal, curvature and shape index, at $1\times$, $2\times$ and $4\times$ the sensor footprint. In total
the benchmark comprises 17{,}522 touch locations and 876{,}100 tactile frames across all 1000
ObjectFolder objects. Objects 1--950 form the training split and objects 951--1000 the evaluation
split.

We define two benchmark settings.

\emph{Ground-truth retrieval benchmark.} Each item is a pair of touches, one designated query and
one designated reference, with their tactile videos and normal renderings. Since the pairing
is given, this setting isolates the coarse alignment and refinement stages from retrieval error.
We simulate 8 reference and 8 query touches per object, giving 16{,}000 touch locations, with the
query touches perturbed by up to $\pm15^\circ$ of in-plane rotation relative to the references.
The evaluation split contains 400 query touches.

\emph{Full pipeline benchmark.} Here we evaluate the complete pipeline including retrieval. We
collect $K$ touches per object, on average 15.2 touches on each of 100 objects, 1{,}522
locations in total, with up to $\pm30^\circ$ of rotation jitter. We then hold out four touches per
object as queries, three when an object has fewer than nine touches, drawn with a fixed random
seed. This gives 396 query touches evaluated against 1{,}121 reference touches. For each query we
retrieve against the remaining touches of that object to pick a reference, align, and refine with
the same pre-trained network.

\paragraph{Baselines.}
Since the task is new, we adapt techniques from prior work in tactile perception.

\emph{Tactile normal quilting}, adapted from Tactile DreamFusion~\cite{tactiledreamfusion}, uses
image quilting to tile the tactile normal of the reference touch across the entire object mesh
surface, renders the view-space normal at the query sensor pose, integrates the normals into a
height field, and renders a tactile video with Taxim~\cite{taxim}.

\emph{Implicit neural representations}, adapted from ObjectFolder~\cite{objectfolder}, train a
NeRF-like implicit representation that takes pixel coordinates and pressing depth as input and
outputs the tactile normal. The representation is fitted per sample on the reference touches and
evaluated at the query locations.

We report PSNR, SSIM and LPIPS, averaged over all frames of all evaluation touches.

\subsection{Performance Comparison}

\subsubsection{Ground-truth retrieval}

\input{tables/tab_gt_retrieval}

Table~\ref{tab:gt_retrieval} reports quantitative results on the ground-truth retrieval benchmark,
separately for the coarse transfer produced by feature-matching alignment alone and for the final
refined transfer. Fig.~\ref{fig:gt_retrieval} shows qualitative results at a range of touch
locations: for each row we show the reference touch, the reference and query normal renderings,
the coarse transfer, our refined prediction, and the ground-truth query touch.

\input{figures/fig_gt_retrieval}

Both stages of our method matter, and both beat the baselines. The coarse transfer alone already
reaches 22.90\,dB, which is 4.6\,dB above the strongest baseline, the implicit neural
representation, and 8.3\,dB above tactile normal quilting. This says that simply finding the right
geometric correspondence between two normal renderings and warping the reference video through it
recovers most of what the query touch looks like, without any learning on the tactile signal
itself. The refinement network then adds a further 8.3\,dB, halving LPIPS from 0.2102 to 0.1085.
The two contributions are complementary rather than redundant: the warp places the tactile pattern
in the right position, and the network supplies the gel dynamics and cleans up the residual
misalignment that the warp leaves behind.

The gap to the baselines is largest on LPIPS, the metric most sensitive to perceptual structure.
Quilting synthesises a plausible tactile texture but has no mechanism to make it agree with the
particular surface under the sensor, so it scores 0.4833. The implicit representation is fitted
per sample and therefore reproduces the reference touches it was trained on, but it must
extrapolate to an unseen location from pixel coordinates alone, which is exactly where the
geometric context our method uses is decisive.

\subsubsection{Full pipeline}

\input{tables/tab_full_pipeline}

Table~\ref{tab:full_pipeline} reports results on the full pipeline benchmark, which includes
retrieval error, again split into coarse and refined transfer.
Fig.~\ref{fig:full_pipeline} compares our predictions against the baselines frame by frame.

\input{figures/fig_full_pipeline}

The ordering of the methods is unchanged when retrieval is folded into the system under test, and
our refined prediction again leads on every metric, at 31.58\,dB, 0.9182 SSIM and 0.1212 LPIPS.
Two differences from the previous setting are worth drawing out.

First, our refined result does not degrade relative to the ground-truth retrieval setting, despite
the larger rotation jitter and despite reference touches now being chosen automatically. Retrieval
is therefore not the bottleneck: when many touches of the same object are available, picking a
usable one is comparatively easy, and the difficulty lies in the alignment and refinement that
follow.

Second, the margin of our coarse transfer over the implicit neural representation nearly vanishes,
21.60\,dB against 21.11\,dB, even though the same comparison was 4.6\,dB in the ground-truth
retrieval setting. The reason is that this benchmark provides many reference touches per object,
which is the regime the per-object implicit representation is designed for, whereas the coarse
transfer still uses exactly one retrieved reference. Our refined prediction nonetheless remains
10.5\,dB ahead, so the advantage does not come from having a better geometric warp alone but from
combining that warp with a network that has learned how the gel behaves.

\subsection{Ablation Study}

\input{tables/tab_ablation}

We ablate the key components of our method to show their contribution to accurate tactile
transfer. All ablations are run on a 20-object subset of the full pipeline benchmark, and
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} reports PSNR, SSIM and LPIPS. We study three groups of choices.

First, the \emph{modality} used for retrieval and for feature matching during initial alignment,
with the field of view fixed at $4\times$ the sensor footprint: surface normals (our default),
RGB colour, curvature, and height map. Second, the \emph{field of view} used for retrieval and
matching: $1\times$, $2\times$ and $4\times$. Third, the components of the refinement stage: no
neural refinement at all (i.e.\ the coarse transfer), no temporal FiLM conditioning, and no normal
map concatenation. Fig.~\ref{fig:ablation} compares predictions from the last three variants
visually.

\input{figures/fig_ablation}

\paragraph{Modality.}
Surface normals are the best of the four modalities on all three metrics, but the spread is
narrow, 1.7\,dB between the best and the worst, and only 0.010 in LPIPS. Every modality here
encodes the same underlying surface, so the choice affects how reliably the matcher fires rather
than what there is to match. Normals are nonetheless a sensible default: they carry the local
orientation the matcher keys on most directly, and unlike RGB they are unaffected by the object's
appearance.

\paragraph{Field of view.}
The three fields of view disagree about which is best depending on the metric, and we do not read
a clear winner from them. Matching at $1\times$ gives the highest PSNR, 0.8\,dB above $4\times$,
while $4\times$ gives the best SSIM and LPIPS. This is consistent with what the two ends trade
off. A narrow field of view sees only the contact patch, so when it matches at all it matches
precisely, which PSNR rewards. A wide field of view sees the surrounding surface, which makes the
match more likely to be found and better conditioned but spreads the correspondences over pixels
the tactile video never covers. We keep $4\times$ as the default because the perceptual metrics
favour it and because the wider context is what makes retrieval reliable.

\paragraph{Refinement.}
This is where the largest effects are. Adding the refinement network to the coarse transfer gains
3.7\,dB and cuts LPIPS almost in half, from 0.2797 to 0.1522. Removing the temporal FiLM
conditioning costs 1.5\,dB and raises LPIPS to 0.1735, confirming that the network genuinely uses
the timestamp: without it, a single static normal map has to explain frames at every stage of the
press, and the network can only predict something close to their average. Removing the normal map
concatenation is by far the most damaging variant, at 25.38\,dB, which falls below even the
unrefined coarse transfer on PSNR while remaining better on LPIPS. We caution against reading the
size of this last gap too literally. The available checkpoint for this arm differs from the full
model in its training schedule as well as in its conditioning, so the number mixes the two effects
and overstates the contribution of concatenation alone. The direction is nevertheless clear, and
it is the direction one would expect: with no view of the query geometry the network has nothing
to tell it which parts of the warped input are wrong.

\subsection{Application in 3D Surface Reconstruction and Visuo-tactile Sensor Simulation}

\input{figures/fig_recon}

Our method predicts tactile surface normals rather than an opaque appearance signal, so its
output can be turned into geometry with no additional supervision. We convert the predicted
normals into per-pixel surface slopes and integrate them into a heightmap with a Poisson solver,
subtracting a least-squares plane fit since the integration is defined only up to a plane. The
result is a 3D reconstruction of the object surface at a location that was never physically
touched. Such reconstructions let an embodied agent reason about fine-grained shape properties of
previously untouched regions, and can drive haptic rendering.

Going one step further, feeding the same heightmap through Taxim's calibrated optical
model~\cite{taxim} produces the colour image a camera-based tactile sensor would have measured,
that is, a virtual visuo-tactile measurement. This could aid manipulation policies that are trained
directly on visuo-tactile sensor readings: an agent could, for instance, rehearse a grasp by
predicting the tactile videos at candidate contact points before touching the object at all.
Fig.~\ref{fig:recon} shows the full chain, from reference touch through predicted tactile normal
video to the reconstructed 3D relief and the simulated colour frames. Both steps after the network
are deterministic post-processing of the predicted normals, so the whole chain runs at deployment
time from a single reference touch.

\subsection{Runtime Analysis}

We report the runtime of our full pipeline, measured on an RTX 3090 GPU as the mean of ten timed
repeats after a warm-up pass. Given $N$ reference touches and one query, producing a touch
prediction takes 0.08\,ms for the retrieval phase after DINOv3 feature extraction, 0.021\,s for
coarse alignment after local feature matching, and 8.5\,ms per frame for neural refinement, which
is 0.42\,s for a 50-frame touch video. The two feature extraction costs excluded from these
figures are 0.06\,s for DINOv3 and 0.08\,s for local feature matching, both of which are single
forward passes of frozen pre-trained networks. End to end, a complete prediction therefore takes
roughly 0.6\,s, of which the refinement network is by far the largest share.

The pipeline also scales gracefully with the size of the reference set. Repeating the measurement
on the largest object in the benchmark, with 27 reference touches instead of 6, changes the three
stage timings to 0.07\,ms, 0.019\,s and 8.6\,ms per frame, which is within measurement noise.
Retrieval is the only stage whose cost depends on $N$ at all, and because the reference features
are computed once and cached it reduces to a single matrix--vector product. Thanks to these short
runtimes our method is lightweight and well suited to runtime-critical applications such as AR/VR
and robotics.
