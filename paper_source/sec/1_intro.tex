\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\input{figures/fig_teaser}

Humans have a remarkable ability to imagine what it would feel like to touch an object at a
particular location. Looking at a ceramic mug, we already know that its glazed side will feel
smooth and that the unglazed ring at its base will feel rough and gritty, long before our fingers
arrive there. This ability
matters: it lets us plan a grasp, avoid an obstacle, and build a holistic picture of the
fine-grained surface detail of a scene without having to physically touch every location.
Crucially, the touch we imagine is not a single snapshot in time. It is a mental simulation of a
process --- of how our soft, non-rigid skin would progressively deform as it presses into the
surface and then withdraws.

Why is this possible at all? Because tactile information is highly repetitive and highly
predictable. Surfaces are made of a small number of materials and a small number of recurring
geometric motifs, so a handful of touches already reveals most of what the rest of an object will
feel like. What remains is to work out \emph{which} previously felt sensation applies at a new
location, and \emph{how} it should be transformed to account for the change in surface geometry.

In this paper we aim to teach machines the same capacity. We introduce the task of finding
\emph{tactile analogies}: given a set of reference touches captured on an object during contact,
predict the tactile signal at new, untouched locations (Fig.~\ref{fig:teaser}). We represent a
touch as a \emph{tactile normal video}, a sequence of frames that encodes the surface normals of
the sensor's deformable gel over the duration of a press. Tactile normal videos are easy to
obtain in practice, because the mapping from the raw colour measurements of a modern vision-based
tactile sensor to gel normals is by design nearly one-to-one; sensor manufacturers such as
GelSight~\cite{gelsight} ship colour-to-normal conversion as part of their software interface. To
predict gel normals at a new location, we render surface normal maps at both the reference and
query sensor poses over multiple fields of view, and compare these renderings to recover a
mapping that transfers reference tactile videos to the query location.

This is, in spirit, the \emph{analogy-based synthesis} idea of the seminal Image
Analogies work~\cite{imageanalogies}: observe a transformation on one pair of images, then apply
the same transformation to a new input. Our setting differs in what plays each role. The observed
side is the surface geometry --- the normal map rendered at each sensor pose --- while the side we
must synthesise is a high-dimensional tactile video rather than a still image. In other words, we
ask for an analogy between geometry and the temporal signal that contact with that geometry
produces.

Several things make this hard. First, the physics of contact is complicated: the gel deforms
non-rigidly, its response depends on material properties that are not observable from geometry
alone, and the deformation evolves over the press. Second, the search space is large. We must
decide which of the available reference touches is relevant \emph{and} how that reference should
be mapped onto the query sensor pose, and these two decisions interact. Third, there is no
large-scale dataset of paired touches at controlled locations on which a model could simply be
trained end to end.

Our solution addresses these in turn. To bridge reference and query locations we use normal
renderings at multiple scales, which give a common, appearance-invariant representation of the
local surface. To tame the search space we adopt a coarse-to-fine estimation scheme. For coarse
alignment we run pre-trained local feature matchers on the normal renderings. Although these
matchers were trained on multi-view photographs and normal maps are well outside that
distribution, we find that they match reliably; the resulting correspondences give a homography
between the reference and query normal maps, which we then use to warp the reference tactile
video and produce an initial transfer. For refinement we train a network that takes the initially
warped tactile normal video and regresses the ground-truth query video. This network is
responsible for the part that geometry alone cannot supply: the subtle dynamics of gel
deformation, and the correction of residual misalignment left by the coarse step.

In experiments on a benchmark we build from the ObjectFolder dataset~\cite{objectfolder}, our
pipeline outperforms every baseline we test, all of which are adapted from prior work on tactile
prediction. It also runs quickly, which matters for the applications we have in mind. Predicted
tactile normals can be integrated into heightmaps for fine-grained 3D surface reconstruction, and
can be pushed back through an optical tactile simulator to produce the colour image a
visuo-tactile sensor would have measured. Together these let an embodied agent reason about
surface detail --- and even rehearse a grasp against simulated sensor readings --- before making
physical contact.

\vspace{0.5em}
\noindent Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
  \item We introduce the task of tactile analogies --- predicting tactile videos at untouched
        locations from a set of reference touches --- and a benchmark for it built on
        ObjectFolder.
  \item We propose a simple and efficient coarse-to-fine solution that combines geometry-based
        retrieval, feature-matching alignment, and a lightweight refinement network.
  \item We show that our method outperforms baselines adapted from prior tactile prediction work.
  \item We demonstrate applications in 3D surface reconstruction and in simulating visuo-tactile
        sensor measurements without physical contact.
\end{itemize}
